What they did, and what happened to them

Lives Sheriffs and farmers, Masons and turpentine country, a nine-year manhunt and a farm sold off the courthouse steps — and the parts of this record that are hard to read.

A note on how this page is written. It was asked for straight, including the parts that reflect badly. So the difficult material is at the top rather than buried at the bottom, stated plainly, with the source for each claim.

Two things are avoided throughout. Nothing is softened — where an ancestor took part in something indefensible, it says so in ordinary words. And nothing is embroidered — where the record is thin or the identification uncertain, that is marked, because a wrongly attributed crime is its own kind of dishonesty.

The hard chapters

Four findings, in descending order of how much they weigh.

The sheriff sold people at the courthouse door

James Malcolm McKinnon Sr. · Sheriff of Coffee County, 1856–1858

Your fourth-great-grandfather held the office of sheriff, and one of the sheriff's duties in Georgia in 1856 was to conduct sales of property to satisfy debts and settle estates. In this state, in that decade, that property included human beings.

His sale notices survive in the Milledgeville paper. They name people: "One negro man, Backus, about thirty-five years old." "One negro woman, Ar'sey, 24 or 25 years old, and her two children."

There is no softening available here and none is offered. Backus and Ar'sey were real people with names we now know, and a man in your direct line stood on the courthouse steps and sold them. Ar'sey's two children were sold with her, which in the arithmetic of those sales was mercy, and was not mercy at all.

Source: sheriff's sale notices, 1856–57, located through the Georgia Historic Newspapers archive. County offices confirmed in Ward, History of Coffee County (1930), "List of Coffee County Officers Dating From 1854."

One household in this tree is documented holding enslaved people

Susan Harper · Irwin County · 1860 slave schedule

The 1860 Irwin County slave schedule records Susan Harper, page 353B, lines 21–22, holding two men — one aged 33, recorded as mulatto, and one aged 30. No slave houses were recorded for her. The free schedule identifies her: 75 years old, born South Carolina, living alone but for a young woman and a child, with $500 in land and $2,485 in personal property.

She is probably the widow of Leonard Harper, the first Harper in Irwin County — the right generation, the right birthplace, the only adult Harper of that age in the county. That identification is not certain, and it is marked as probable rather than asserted.

Source: 1860 U.S. Census Slave Schedule, Irwin County, Georgia, read page by page. The 1845 probate of Leonard Harper's will is recorded in Clements, History of Irwin County (1932).

The 1838 militia, and what it was for

James Malcolm McKinnon Sr. · Capt. Levi J. Knight's Company · 15 August – 15 October 1838

The muster roll says it in its own words: a company raised "on a sudden emergency to repel the invasion of the Indians into that county in the year 1838."

1838 is the year of the Cherokee removal and the Trail of Tears. In south Georgia it was the aftermath of the Creek war and the tail of the Second Seminole War. The Creek people of Georgia had been forced out in 1836–37; bands remaining around the Okefenokee raided frontier settlements, and settlers were killed. The danger was real and the men who enlisted experienced it as defence.

And the larger frame is not in dispute. These companies operated in the service of clearing Native people off land the state was already handing out to white settlers by lottery. The ground Knight's men were defending in Lowndes County had been taken from the Creek Nation eighteen months earlier. Serving in that company meant taking up arms on the removal side of a war of dispossession. Both things are true, and the second does not cancel the first.

Source: "Muster Roll of Capt. Levi J. Knight's Independent Company from Lowndes county," published by Folks Huxford in The South Georgia Historical and Genealogical Quarterly, transcribed from the records at the state capitol.

They enlisted for the Confederacy — one company, a month before secession

The Berrien Minute Men · enrolled 28 November 1860

Georgia did not secede until January 1861. On 28 November 1860, a company was already enrolled in Berrien County as "Capt. Levi J. Knight's Company of Volunteers Styled, The Berrien Minute Men." Five Lukes were on the original roll, including David Perry Luke and his brother Jasper M. Luke, the former coroner of Irwin County.

The company became Companies D and G of the 29th Georgia Infantry. They served on the Georgia coast, then fought at Chickamauga in September 1863, where their brigade carried 1,200 men into the fight and lost 99 killed, 426 wounded and 80 missing, and then through the Atlanta campaign.

The through-line is a single man. Levi J. Knight captained the 1838 Indian-removal company, held seven human beings in 1860, and raised the company the Lukes enlisted in a month before secession. He is not your ancestor. He is the hinge that all three of these chapters turn on.

Three more of your kin are on the Confederate rolls, and what happened to them is recorded: John P. Ricketson, Co. I of the 26th Georgia — captured, held at Point Lookout, Maryland, and wounded four times. Vincent Ricketson — leg amputated after being shot at Trevilian Station, Virginia, 11 June 1864. Mark McKinnon — enlisted 1863, left the command at Savannah in September 1864 "crippled and deprived hand," and was rejected for a pension in 1911.

Sources: Berrien Minute Men muster roll; Georgia Confederate Pension Applications, Georgia Archives RG 58-1-1; Ward's roster of Coffee County Confederate soldiers.

And the finding that runs the other way, which is also real. Every page of the 1850 and 1860 slave schedules was read for eight counties — Irwin, Berrien, Coffee, Telfair, Clinch, Lowndes, Ware and Appling. No person surnamed Luke appears as a slaveholder anywhere in any of them. Neither does Ricketson, Yawn, Graves, Mercer, Cribb or Story.

That is a documented absence from a complete record, not a failure to find. It was verified: the schedules read total exactly to the published census aggregates, so nothing was missed.

John Luke's $760 in personal property could not have bought one adult. The clearest proof of the point is Henry S. C. Harper, who in 1860 held $2,235 in personal property and no enslaved people at all — while Susan Harper, in the same county the same year, held $2,485 and two men. Nearly identical estates, opposite records. In this country personal property meant cattle. Wealth was not a proxy for slaveholding in either direction.

Context, honestly given: Irwin County was 14.5% enslaved in 1860 and Berrien 12.4%, against 43.7% for Georgia as a whole. Irwin had 44 slaveholders in the entire county. This was poor herding country, and most families in it held no one. That is an explanation, not an excuse — and it makes Coffee County, at 23%, and Telfair, at 30.8%, stand out sharply.

Eight things that happened

Out of roughly fifteen thousand newspaper pages.

A nine-year manhunt that began on a McKinnon farm

9 July 1901 – 10 November 1910 · Coffee County

A labourer named Jake DeVoss shot and killed Charles McKinnon and wounded James McKinnon seven miles south of Douglas. The governor posted $200; James McKinnon added his own money, and the reward sat in the Union Bank for years while he circulated a head-to-toe description of the fugitive.

Sheriff David Ricketson ran DeVoss down in Reeves, Louisiana, in May 1910 — nine years later. He was hanged at Douglas on 10 November 1910. Two of your families, on opposite sides of the same case.

A stabbing at the Lax school picnic

Friday night, 28 September 1900 · Lax, Coffee County

The Douglas Breeze ran it under the headline "A Closing of a School Breaks up in a Row and Campaign Whiskey the Cause of a Fatal Tragedy." The public term at Lax school had ended and been marked with a picnic dinner.

Riley Luke, son of Ben Luke — almost certainly R. B. Luke — was stabbed in the back below the left shoulder blade in a general cutting affray and bled to death hours later before the doctor could reach him. John B. Graham was jailed for it, and acquitted that November.

Identification as R. B. Luke's son is probable, not proven — "Ben Luke near Lax" fits and no second Luke household there appears, but no record states it outright.

"A barrel of Lemonade setting in the gate"

Fourth of July, 1912 · the home of Uncle Jim McKinnon

The best single document in this whole project. A correspondent drove up at eleven o'clock and noticed "on every side a fine crop such as dont grow on every mans place." There was a melon cutting that "supplied the whole crowd and plenty left," and "a barrel of Lemonade setting in the gate with about a dozen dippers that meant help yourself." At one o'clock dinner was "spread out under the large oaks on a long table."

It ends with a line that tells you more about your third-great-grandfather than any census could: "some one will have to read this little notice for uncle Jim for he never reads news papers nor anything else but the bible or hymn book."

Seven years later the same paper noted that Mrs. McKinnon called at the office and renewed the subscription. Mary Jane Durham McKinnon was the reader in that marriage.

The Douglas Enterprise, 13 July 1912, p.1, and 17 January 1919, p.5.

Sick three days

21 June 1920 · Lucius L. Luke, aged 48

"Yesterday the remains of L. L. Luke, a prominent farmer of Irwin county were laid to rest at Brushy Creek church. The deceased was only sick for three days, when death overtook him. A large concourse of friends and neighbors attended his funeral."

His death certificate, read directly, gives the cause: an intestinal obstruction, about three days. Two independent records, one death — and the certificate is reproduced in full on the documents page.

His headstone reads "An honest man, the noblest work of GOD" beneath a Masonic emblem.

The Leader, Enterprise and Press (Fitzgerald), 23 June 1920, p.5.

Four hundred and ninety acres, sold off the courthouse steps

1922, 1923, and again in 1924 · the estate of L. L. Luke

Two years after Lucius died, the sheriff advertised "All of lot of land No. 261 in 5th land district said county, containing 490 acres… levied on and to be sold as the property of Mrs. L. L. Luke to satisfy a tax fifa… for State and County Taxes for the year 1921."

The following year the same 490 acres were levied again, for the taxes of 1922. And in December 1924 a creditor came for the machinery: a Frick Eclipse steam engine and boiler on iron wheels — a sawmill or threshing rig — seized from Narcissus Luke as administratrix of her husband's estate.

This was not simple bad luck. The boll weevil reached Georgia in 1915, and state cotton acreage collapsed from a record 5.2 million acres in 1914 to 2.6 million by 1923. The Lukes were inside a regional agricultural failure. The newspaper page carrying Lucius's obituary was already running advertisements for calcium arsenate — the weevil poison of that exact moment — under the line "too late to kick after the crop is gone."

The Douglas Enterprise, June 1922 and December 1924; Coffee County Progress, October–November 1923.

The reunion that maps the whole family

18 July 1914 · R. B. Luke's residence near Lax

One paragraph in the Douglas Enterprise does what a census cannot. The guests at R. B. Luke's reunion came from Douglas, Ambrose, Ocilla, Alapaha and Willacoochee — the entire Luke network across four counties, in a single list of names.

He did it again the following July. That notice ends: "A prayer and praise meeting came toward the close, and every one left feeling that they had spent a good day."

Thirty-six years later the 1950 census found his grandson Jack on a farm on the Lax–Willacoochee road. Three generations on one stretch of country.

The sheriff who died at forty miles an hour

Saturday 9 March 1918 · David Ricketson

He was driving a Ford out of Broxton toward Fitzgerald, swerved around a buggy, and rolled the car. The windshield frame crushed his chest. He spoke once on the way home and never again, dying at a quarter past nine that night. Broxton and Douglas Masons took the body in charge. He was a charter member of the Douglas Elks — and the man who had caught Jake DeVoss in Louisiana eight years earlier.

The doctor who taught school three months at a time

1924 · Dr. George M. Ricketson

The Enterprise ran his whole life story after he won a seat in the legislature by 551 votes. He had taught school three months at a stretch to pay his own way, bought 267 acres on credit, then became a doctor, then went to the capitol.

The paper added that he had to be talked into having his picture made.

On the lodges. In a county where four adults in ten could not read, the Masonic lodge was the main institution of mutual aid outside the church — burial benefits, care of widows and orphans, and a network of trust that crossed county lines. The same names recur as lodge officers, grand jury foremen and county officials. The emblem on a headstone was a declaration of standing.

R. B. Luke's lodge was most likely Lax No. 556 and Lucius's most likely Ocilla No. 374, but neither membership is individually documented — both are inference from residence, and are marked as such.

So when John and Telitha Luke were marked illiterate in 1860, they were in the majority of their neighbours, not the exception. In the 1850 Irwin census, every adult Luke male in the county is marked unable to read and write — Jasper, David P., Daniel M., Jesse J., Mack, John, Joshua A. and James. So are the Harper men.

Then watch what happens over four generations:

  • 1860 — John and Telitha Luke, both marked illiterate.
  • 1912 — Jim McKinnon reads the Bible and the hymn book, and nothing else.
  • 1919 — Mary Jane McKinnon walks into the newspaper office and renews her own subscription.
  • 1920 — Lucius Luke is buried under a literary epitaph.
  • 1940s — Versa Mae Luke wins a scholarship to the Martha Berry School.
  • 2026 — a descendant is in graduate school, reading this.

What the work actually was

So that "farmer" means something.

Before 1870 — open range

They fenced the fields, not the animals. Cattle and hogs ran loose in unfenced pine woods, identified by ear-marks, and each spring the range was burned off to green the wiregrass. Corn, sweet potatoes, peas and cane were grown for the house rather than the market. There were no towns to trade in — men went to Savannah or the river landings in groups with pack horses. A "farmer in Irwin County in 1880" was, mostly, a herdsman.

1869 onward — the pines come down

The railroad crossed northern Coffee County in 1869, and the sawmills and turpentine stills followed it. Within a few years, in the county historian's words, "nearly the whole of Coffee County was overrun with turpentine stills."

The turpentine year ran in seasons: boxing the trees in winter — a crop was about 10,000 boxed trees — then chipping the bark as it warmed, then dipping the gum with a trowel-shaped spade through spring and summer. Superintendents and woodsriders were normally white; most woodsmen were African American. Fires at the stills were frequent and often seriously injured the workers. Every two or three years the crew dismantled the still and the commissary and moved on to fresh timber.

Georgia led the nation from 1890. Florida led from 1905 to 1923. Nealie Hinton was born in Levy County, Florida in 1906 and her family stayed until about 1929 — the whole of Florida's turn at the top.

1915 onward — the weevil, and then wages

The boll weevil arrived in 1915 and halved Georgia's cotton acreage inside a decade. That is the context for the Luke tax sales. By 1950 the census pages around this family show the new world arriving alongside the old: "logging trees / saw mill," "delivers ice / Ice plant," "teaching agriculture / Vocational School," "Filling Stat."

That is the doorway to the twentieth-century jobs in this file — Fitzgerald Underwear, Fleetwood Homes, Elixir Industries, Spectrum, Sears, the Savannah shipyards. A farm-and-timber county became a wage-work county within one lifetime. Versa Mae Luke building bomb casings in Savannah and Willie Mae McKinnon spending thirty years at Spectrum are the same story from two ends.
