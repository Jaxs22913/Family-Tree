Where the research goes next

What to ask for Nine records that would answer the questions this tree cannot answer from a screen — the free routes first, then who holds the rest, what it costs, and the exact words to ask for it.

4 free sources first then $25–$70 a record Two of these would close standing questions

The wall in this research is filming, not effort. Almost everything unanswered here is unanswered because the record was never digitised — not because it is lost. Georgia death certificates are indexed online only for 1919–1927, and Atkinson County's marriage books were never microfilmed at all. The documents exist. They are simply on paper, in four buildings.

Correction to something said earlier in this project. The online index stops in 1927, but the records do not. Georgia Vital Records holds death certificates from January 1919 to the present, and the Georgia Archives holds the originals through 1943. Several ancestors whose certificates were written off as out of range are in fact obtainable by post.

FREE

Do these before you spend anything

no fee, no trip

Four of the questions on this page have already been moved forward without a cheque. Everything below costs nothing, and two of them have produced findings that are on this site now. If money is the constraint, this is the section that matters.

Georgia Historic Newspapersfree · no login

The single most productive free source in this whole project, and it was sitting unused because the page images are behind a bot check.

The scanned pages are protected, but the full transcribed text is served openly through the site’s search, which returns the complete text of every matching page. So the archive is fully readable even though the images are not. Coverage runs from the 1800s into the 1990s depending on the paper, and the Coffee County titles — the Douglas Enterprise and the Coffee County Progress — are both in it.

What it has already produced A daughter of R. B. Luke and Jane Yawn that no record in this file knew about — from one sentence in a country column in 1912.

The full guest list of the 1914 family reunion at Lax, which names eight households of relatives across four towns.

The first documented facts about Elias Carver — school patron in 1912, juror in 1924 and 1926 — for a man who until now had almost nothing but a headstone.
Where to search next — the country columns, not the news. Rocky Pond, Lax, Mora, Ambrose and Willacoochee each had a correspondent who reported who visited whom. Those sentences name relationships that appear in no official record anywhere.

Folks Huxford, History of Clinch County (1916)free full text

Huxford is the standard authority on these families. His famous multi-volume Pioneers of Wiregrass Georgia is still in copyright — but his 1916 county history is not, and it is on the Internet Archive in full.

Searching it turned up Joseph Ricketson, Justice of the Peace of the 586th or Mud Creek District, commissioned 14 January 1841 — the same district where Serena Ricketson farmed as a widow and where James M. McKinnon was enumerated in 1840. That is the previously unnamed generation between Joseph who died in 1820 and John P. who was born in 1828.

Worth trying the same way — other out-of-copyright county histories for Coffee, Irwin, Berrien and Ware. The Irwin County history (Clements, 1932) is already in use here and came from the same place.

Find a Gravefree

Memorials often carry a transcribed obituary and links to parents — which is most of what a $25 certificate would tell you, at no cost.

Worth checking for every person on the priority list above, but especially Elias Carver and Laura Story at Carver Baptist Church, and Joe Edd Giddens and Sallie Mae Mercer at Sweetwater. If a contributor has posted an obituary, it will name the parents and save you the fee. It is not a primary record and nothing from it should be promoted to confirmed on its own — but it tells you whether the certificate is worth buying.

A free FamilySearch accountfree · registration only

The 1850–1940 censuses, and Georgia county marriage books, at no charge.

This is the cheapest way to reach the 1850 census of Laurens County, which is the most valuable page left unread in this file — Lukes, Graveses and Yawns were all in that county in that decade. Most public libraries also provide Ancestry Library Edition free on their own machines, which covers the same ground.

Three things that will get a request rejected. The form requires the county of death — a state-wide search will not be run for you, so give the county even if you are guessing, and say in the margin which other counties to try. Fees are non-refundable: if the record is not there you receive a "not on file" letter and the $25 is spent. And access is limited to people with a direct and tangible interest, so state your relationship explicitly on the form — great-grandson, great-great-grandson — rather than leaving it blank.

Counties to put on each form Jane Yawn Luke (1943) — try Atkinson first, then Coffee, then Berrien.
Martha Ricketson McKinnon (1928) — Coffee, then Atkinson.
George Albert McKinnon (1932) — Coffee, then Atkinson.
Narcisus Harper Luke (1940) — Irwin, then Coffee.
Elias Carver (1954) and Laura Story (1958) — Coffee.
Joe Edd Giddens (1974) and Sallie Mae Mercer (1992) — Atkinson.
Cheaper alternative — the county vital records office where the death happened often holds the same certificate, and is usually faster and less expensive than the state office. Worth one phone call to Coffee or Atkinson before posting eight forms to Atlanta.

The important part: you do not need to be next of kin. Because the file is archival, anyone may request it and no signature from the veteran or the family is required. That removes the obstacle this question has been sitting behind. Give his full name, date of birth (12 January 1937), and state that you believe he served in the U.S. Army in the 1950s; a service number is helpful but not required.

Free first — the Coffee County Clerk of Superior Court recorded discharge papers locally at no charge, and a recorded DD-214 would give you the unit and dates without the $70.

The Archives states plainly that it does not do research by mail, email or telephone, and does not make copies for private individuals. Staff will point you at a collection; they will not pull a document for you. So this is a drive and a day in a reading room, or an independent researcher hired through the Georgia Genealogical Society.

If you are making the trip anyway, Martha Ricketson (1928), George Albert McKinnon (1932) and Narcisus Harper (1940) are all in that range, and reading them at Morrow costs nothing against $75 by post.
5

Rose, Tom, and the third boy

the hardest search on this site

Finding them after freedomthe one that matters most

Leonard Harper’s 1842 will names three people his family owned — a woman named Rose, a boy named Tom left to Henry S. C. Harper, and a third boy whose name cannot be read. Everything below is about what became of them after 1865.

The difficulty is not squeamishness, it is the records. Before 1870 no federal census named an enslaved person. After 1865 a freedperson could take any surname, and most of the record sets built to catch them — the Freedmen’s Bureau above all — barely reached the wiregrass. So the search is narrow and it has to be done by hand.

Already checked — do not repeat these 1870 census, Irwin County, Black Harpers. Seven people, one household: Joseph Harper 40, Margaret 31, and five children. Joseph’s age matches the younger man Susannah Harper held in 1860. No Rose. No Tom.

1870 census, Irwin County, by given name across every surname — the right way to search, since freedpeople often took a name that was not their holder’s. Ten Black and Mulatto men named Thomas: born 1805, 1851, 1862, 1863, 1864, 1865, 1867, 1869. A boy bequeathed in 1842 would be roughly 33 to 43 in 1870, and not one of them is. Three women named Rose or Rosa, all children born in the 1850s and later. Neither is in the county.

Freedmen’s Bureau labour contracts. Name-indexed and searchable — and all fifteen Georgia Harpers in it are at Savannah and Griffin, which is where the field offices were. The Bureau never reached Irwin County. The Georgia field office records are browse-only images, not a name index, so they cannot be searched at all.

1850 slave schedule, Irwin County. Susannah Harper is not in it, under her name or an initial — a checked negative, not a gap: the county is certainly indexed, with five Paulk households on the same pages.

1 · The 1867 voter returns, on Ancestryfree at any public library

The best untried search, and it costs nothing.

In 1867 the U.S. Army registered voters across Georgia under the Reconstruction Acts, and for the first time that meant registering Black men by name, county by county. FamilySearch has this collection but its index carries only a name, a date and a certificate number — no county, no race, which makes it useless for this. Ancestry’s version of the same record carries name, race and residence.

What to search Georgia, U.S., Returns of Qualified Voters and Reconstruction Oath Books, 1867–1869
Filter to Irwin County and read every Black registrant. Tom would be about thirty to forty in 1867 and eligible. Any surname — he need not still be a Harper.

Worth doing for Coffee County in the same sitting: the Harpers spread there, and the 1870 census puts thirty-seven of them in the county.

2 · Irwin County estate records, at Morrowfree to view

The document that would name all three of them again, with values.

The will gave Susannah a life estate — so nothing was divided in 1843 and the people stayed with her. What that produces is an inventory and appraisement of Leonard Harper’s estate, and then annual returns filed year after year while she lived. Inventories name enslaved people individually because they are being valued; annual returns repeat them. This is the only record class likely to give Rose and Tom an age, and to follow them past 1843.

The Georgia Archives lists estate records of slaveholders among its county microfilm, and Irwin County’s probate volumes run from 1820. The will itself was found in the deed book, not the probate book — early Georgia counties mixed them — so ask for both.

What to ask the desk for Irwin County, Georgia — estate records, inventories and appraisements, and annual returns, 1843 to 1865. Estate of Leonard Harper, will proven January term 1843, recorded 26 January 1843. Executors Fleming B. Harper and Andrew McClelland; widow and life tenant Susannah Harper.

Same trip, same county: Irwin County tax digests, 1843–1860. Georgia taxed enslaved people by head, so the digests count how many Susannah Harper was assessed for in each year — which brackets when someone left the household, died, or was sold.

The 1880 census has now been searched, and it is negative. Seven Black men named Thomas in Irwin County, born 1862, 1849, 1854, 1876, 1879 and one in 1804 — none in the band a boy of 1842 would fall into. No older Rose at all.

One warning for anyone repeating this. An enumeration district of Irwin County is filed under Chatham County in the 1880 index. Henry S. C. Harper’s own entry is standardised as District 5, Chatham, Georgia while the original reads District 5, ED 58, Irwin, GA. A search filtered to Irwin will not return those people, and a search filtered to Chatham drowns them in Savannah. Filter by birth year range instead — that does work, and it cut one such search from 1,504 results to 197.

A word about what this search is. These three people appear in this family’s records once, as property, in a sentence dividing them between sons. Every item on this page is an attempt to make them appear a second time as themselves. It may not work. That is a reason to try the records that exist, not a reason to stop.

Pushing the McKinnons back to Scotland

all of this can be done online

Where the line actually standsone link missing

Three of the four steps to the water are documented. One is not.

Documented: Telfair County was created in 1807 and “a large number of the earliest white settlers were Scots Highlanders from the Carolinas” — Charles McKinnon’s family crossed from North Carolina before 1808, which the 1850 census fixes (the mother born North Carolina 1775, her son Daniel born Georgia 1808, every child after him Telfair-born) — and James Malcolm McKinnon Sr. was born in North Carolina about 1804, from his own household’s answer in the 1850 census of Ware County.

Missing: any document joining James to Charles’s household. He was about thirty when Charles died, too old to appear in the guardianship bonds that name his five younger children, and no estate distribution naming adult heirs has surfaced.

1 · Search every spelling, not just McKinnonfree — and it is the reason searches keep failing

The single most useful thing on this page. It costs nothing and it changes results immediately.

This family is almost never written “McKinnon” in the records. The 1840 Ware County census enters him as KINON, James M. The 1850 Telfair sheets read Kinnon. FamilySearch titles his own 1850 page James McKennan. And the machine transcription of the Telfair deed books renders his father as Charles W Kinnon, Charles M. Kinnon and Chs M. Kinnon — the Mc read as a W, or an M., or dropped.

Run every search under all of these McKinnon · Kinnon · Kinon · McKennan · McKinnan · Mckinon · Mc Kinnon · Kennon

Searching “Charles McKinnon” in the Telfair books returns almost nothing. Searching “Charles Kinnon” returns thirteen matches in a single deed volume. Same man, same book. That is the whole difference.

2 · Georgia property tax digests, on Ancestryyou already have the account

The best placement test there is, and it is an evening at your own desk.

Georgia taxed by militia district, and a man appears on the digest the year he turns twenty-one. James was born about 1804, so he should first appear about 1825. The Georgia Archives states that digests in its custody up to 1890 are scanned and indexed on Ancestry. The census only names heads of household, which is why 1830 could not answer this — a tax digest names every taxpayer.

What to search Georgia, U.S., Property Tax Digests, 1793–1892
Telfair County, 1825 through 1838, under every spelling above.

If James M. Kinnon is on a Telfair digest in the same militia district as Charles, that is the placement this whole question turns on. If he is on a Ware or Appling digest instead from the start, that is an answer too — it would mean he was never in Telfair and the Charles connection dies, which is worth knowing just as much.

3 · Georgia wills and probate, on Ancestrysame account

Charles’s estate file, if it survives as a file rather than a book entry.
What to search Georgia, U.S., Wills and Probate Records, 1742–1992 — Telfair County, Charles McKinnon / Charles Kinnon, 1833–1840.

What would end the argument is a distribution or division naming the adult heirs. The guardianship bonds of 5 May 1834 name only the five minors — Margarett, David, Cornelius, Alexander and William — because minors are the only ones a court appoints guardians for. A distribution names everyone.

4 · Georgia Historic Newspapersfree, no account at all

Administrators were required by law to advertise. That is a public notice with a name on it.

gahistoricnewspapers.galileo.usg.edu is free and searchable to the word. An estate in Telfair in the 1830s would have advertised in the Milledgeville papers — the Georgia Journal, the Southern Recorder, the Federal Union — because that is where statewide legal notices ran. Citations for letters of administration, notices to debtors and creditors, and advertisements of sale all name the administrator, and often the heirs who applied.

What to search All spellings, 1833 to 1842, using the advanced search so the date range actually applies. Then the same again for Margaret after her death, some time after 1850.

5 · The one that needs a person in the roomnot online

Recorded here because it is the definitive answer, not because you have to be the one to go.

Telfair County Court of Ordinary minutes, 1833–1840 and 1850–1860. The books are digitised and the machine has read them — but a search can only find what the machine read correctly, and these pages defeat it constantly. It has already turned Harper into Hacker, orphan into or plan, and McKinnon into three different things on one page. The estate entries may be sitting there in handwriting the search will never surface. Reading them means turning pages, which means the Georgia Archives at Morrow or an independent researcher hired through the Georgia Genealogical Society.

And once the link is proven, the Scottish side is genuinely reachable. The Highland settlement of the Cape Fear valley of North Carolina — the Argyll Colony, from Argyllshire and Skye, from 1739 and heaviest between 1739 and 1775 — is one of the very few colonial-era emigrations with surviving ship lists and emigrant registers. The North Carolina counties to work through are Cumberland, Moore, Robeson, Richmond, Bladen and Anson. After that: ScotlandsPeople, whose old parish registers reach back into the 1500s for many parishes.

A caution worth carrying. Highland parish registers are patchy, and families who were Catholic or Episcopalian often do not appear in Church of Scotland registers at all. Getting across the water is realistic. Landing on a named parish is not guaranteed.
