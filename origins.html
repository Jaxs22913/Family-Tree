Where the family came from

Origins Not one answer but nine — a different route for every line, and an honest account of where each of them runs out.

Overwhelmingly British Isles. Mostly English, substantially Scottish, probably one Flemish thread — and already American for a century before Georgia existed.

Your ancestors were not immigrants. By the time they reached Irwin County in the 1820s they were Carolina backcountry and coastal-plain people — herders and small farmers who had been on this continent since the colonial period, moving overland from North and South Carolina as each new tract of land opened.

ROUTES

Line by line, as far as the records go

before 1774 – 1930
LUKEyour surname
South Carolina → Georgia, about 1806–1812 → Irwin County by 1825 Proven by census birthplaces. The 1850 Irwin County census shows Daniel Luke's two eldest sons, Mack and Joshua, born in South Carolina about 1803 and 1805 — and every younger sibling born in Georgia from about 1813. That brackets the migration to within a few years. The earliest Luke record anywhere in Georgia is July 1825, when the Irwin County Inferior Court appointed Daniel Luke to mark out a river road; there is no Luke household in the 1820 county census.

A 1932 county history adds that Daniel Luke "moved from South Carolina on Little Peedee River" — the Marion and Horry District country. Every other testable claim in that sketch checks out against independent records, which is why it is worth believing on the one claim that cannot be tested here.
GRAVESTelitha Jane, b. 1819
Brunswick County, North Carolina → Laurens County, Georgia, about 1850 → Berrien County The line that reaches furthest back. Both the 1860 and 1870 censuses record Telitha Jane Graves as born in North Carolina — and so was her sister Rutha Ellen, who married Telitha's own stepson. The two North Carolina sisters lived in adjacent houses in Berrien County.

Behind them, a compiled genealogy traces Benjamin Graves in Brunswick County, North Carolina at the 1790 census, born before 1774. If that holds, he is the only ancestor in this tree documented on American soil before the Revolution ended.
McKINNONyour mother's line
North Carolina, about 1804 → Ware County, Georgia by 1838 → Coffee County And then it stops. James Malcolm McKinnon Sr. was born in North Carolina about 1804, was in the Georgia wiregrass by August 1838, and served as Sheriff of Coffee County in 1856–58. No record names his parents. No North Carolina county, no ship, no immigrant.

A McKinnon born in North Carolina about 1804 is a natural grandchild of the Cape Fear Highland Scots settlement — the Argyll Colony of 1739 and the much larger wave that peaked in 1774, concentrated in Cumberland, Harnett, Hoke and Moore Counties. Neighbouring Telfair County is documented as settled by "Scots Highlanders from the Carolinas."

But the evidence cuts both ways. His family's given names are markedly anglicised — Mark, James, Mary Ann, Wakefield, Georgia Ann; only "Archibald" is characteristically Highland. He married into English-descended Wiregrass families. And the line is Primitive Baptist, where the Cape Fear Scots were famously and durably Presbyterian. Compare the Thomas County McKinnons — Murdock, Duncan, marrying McArthurs and McNairs. That is what an unassimilated Highland line looks like. This one is not it.
HARPERNarcisus, b. 1877
McIntosh County, Georgia → Irwin County between 1820 and 1830 A notably different story from the Lukes'. Leonard Harper, the first Harper in Irwin County, appears in the 1830 census and the 1830 tax digest and is absent from 1820. A 1932 county history says he came from McIntosh County — which is coastal Georgia, not overland from the Carolinas. If so, the Harpers entered Georgia by the sea.

His grandson's wife Nancy Merritt was born in North Carolina about 1817 — the second North Carolina birth in this tree.
YAWNJane, b. 1855
South Carolina → Telfair and Appling Counties, Georgia, 1810s–1820s The most interesting surname in your tree, and nobody has solved it. The standard American reference says flatly: "English: unexplained. This surname is very rare in Britain." It appears in none of the major British surname dictionaries.

What is proven: the 1860 Telfair County census shows James Yawn, 64, and Ephraim Yawn, 50, both born in South Carolina, with all their children Georgia-born. Same wave as the Lukes.

The popular story — that Yawn is an anglicisation of German Jahn, via Swiss-German settlers at Orangeburg, South Carolina in the 1740s — appears only on user-edited pages. It is phonetically unremarkable and Orangeburg genuinely was a German-Palatine settlement, so it is plausible. It is not evidence.

One thing this research can add: measuring the surname across the Georgia newspaper archive puts the Yawn homeland unmistakably in Telfair County — at forty-four times the statewide rate. That is where to look.
RICKETSONJohn P., b. 1828
Montgomery County, Georgia, 4 June 1828 → Coffee County We have this from the man himself. John P. Ricketson swore it twice in his Confederate pension affidavits — born Montgomery County, Georgia, resident of the state continuously "ever since the 4 day of June 1828." He signed each affidavit with his mark.

Behind him, Ricketsons held Georgia headright grants in Richmond, Washington and Franklin Counties in 1784–85, with plainly English given names — Timothy, Jordan, Marmaduke. Montgomery was carved from Washington in 1793, which makes the chain plausible and unproven.

And a caution that matters more than the rest of this page. A note on his memorial says he was the son of Sarah Ricketson and a John Peterson, born before she married. If that is true, the Ricketson name here descends through an unmarried mother and the biological male line behind it is Peterson. Unverified — but it is the sharpest illustration anywhere in this tree of why a surname is not an ancestry.
HINTONNealie, b. 1906
Levy County, Florida → Irwin County, Georgia, between 1929 and mid-1930 The only line that comes into this family from outside Georgia in living memory. Nealie Hinton was born in Levyville, Levy County, Florida, and her two eldest daughters were born there too; her third was born in Irwin County in July 1930. That dates the crossing precisely.

Surname mapping backs it up from an unexpected direction: Hinton is five times rarer in these ten Georgia counties than in Georgia as a whole — it is genuinely not a Wiregrass name. The family was in north Florida across exactly the years Florida led the nation in naval stores production, 1905 to 1923. Whether they worked the turpentine camps is untested, but the timing and the place are right.
OVERSTREETMartha Amanda
Probably Flanders → the Chesapeake or the Carolinas → Georgia The clearest non-English name in the tree. Overstreet is probably an altered form of the Flemish Overstraete — topographic, roughly "across the street." It is absent from the British surname dictionaries entirely. Flemish and Dutch families entered the Chesapeake and the Carolinas in the colonial period, which fits the standard route exactly.
GIDDENS · CRIBB · CARVER · STORY · HALLthe rest
English, and rooted in these counties for generations Giddens — English, habitational, from Giddinge in Kent, Gedding in Suffolk, or the Gidding villages near Huntingdon. Twelve times more concentrated in these counties than chance, and thirty-four times in Berrien.
Cribb — English, from the Old English word for a manger. Rare: about 3,600 bearers in the whole United States, and nine times concentrated here. Its rarity makes it one of the most tractable lines left.
Story — northern English, from the Old Norse personal name Stori. Scandinavian-derived, out of the Danelaw.
Carver — English occupational. Hall — English, someone at the manor hall, and one of the commonest surnames in the language. Neither tells you anything.
CONTEXT

Who settled this country, and why

1739 – 1832

The Wiregrass is the longleaf-pine belt of south Georgia, named for the wiry grass that grew under the pines on sandy soil. Every county in this family sits inside it.

During the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, the forest was settled as part of a larger folk migration of Euro-Americans and enslaved African Americans from the Carolinas and, to a lesser degree, Virginia. Mark V. Wetherington, "Wiregrass Georgia," New Georgia Encyclopedia

They came for cheap land, open range, and few planters — most working fewer than a hundred acres, living by subsistence farming and livestock herding. Of the settlers who stated an origin after Georgia became a royal colony, about two-thirds came from the Carolinas.

The lottery that opened this land

Georgia did not sell its frontier. It gave it away by lottery to people already living in the state. Irwin County — parent of Coffee, Berrien, Ben Hill, Tift, Turner and more — was created in 1818 from land ceded by the Muscogee (Creek) under the Treaty of Fort Jackson, and distributed in the 1820 lottery, in unusually large 490-acre lots.

Two things follow, and both matter for this family. The lottery required three years' Georgia residence, so the people who drew were already in the state — the move into the Wiregrass was the second leg of a migration that began in the Carolinas. And the land being handed out had been taken from the Creek Nation eighteen months before.

The Highland Scots route

The Cape Fear valley of North Carolina received the largest Gaelic-speaking settlement in America — the Argyll Colony of 1739, then a much larger wave peaking in 1774, driven by rents and evictions after the Jacobite defeat of 1745. Gaelic survived among some of them into the mid-nineteenth century. Many later moved south, and Telfair County, Georgia, immediately adjacent to this family's ground, was settled by "Scots Highlanders from the Carolinas."

That is the natural fit for a McKinnon born in North Carolina about 1804. It is not a finding.

What the land did to the people on it

Before 1870 this was open-range stock country, not a cotton economy. Families fenced their fields and let cattle and hogs run loose in the pine woods, identified by ear-marks. You can see it in the numbers: John Luke's personal property in 1860 ($760) exceeded his real estate ($500) — the signature of a man whose wealth walked around on four legs.

Then the railroads came, and after them the sawmills and the turpentine stills. The county historian, remembering it in 1930, did not mince words:

There never has been such a destruction of property as was wrought by the saw mills and turpentine operators… When the mills and the stills had done their work of destruction, then came the cross tie getters and cut every tree big enough to make a cross tie. Ward, History of Coffee County (1930)
HONEST

What a surname can and cannot tell you

A surname's etymology describes a word, not a bloodline. "Hall" was coined independently by hundreds of unrelated medieval English families who happened to live near a manor hall. Knowing what the word means tells you nothing about whether you descend from any particular one of them.

Frequency destroys information. Brown has 1.4 million American bearers. Hall has 407,000. Willis 130,000, Harper 124,000. For those, etymology is worth precisely nothing about your line.

Surnames pass through non-biological channels. In the American South especially, enslaved people took or were given surnames unconnected to ancestry. McKinnon is 24% Black in the 2010 census; McMillan 26%; Hinton 41%. A Scottish surname in Georgia is compatible with several completely different histories.

And a surname is one line in 2n. It traces a single path — father's father's father — through a tree that has 1,024 ancestral slots ten generations back. The twenty-one surnames on this page sound like a lot. They cover a vanishingly small fraction of your actual ancestry.

Clan associations and crest vendors will tell you that you are "of Clan MacKinnon" and sell you a tartan. That is merchandising. It carries no evidentiary weight at all.

What DNA would actually do

An autosomal test will not cleanly separate "English" from "Scottish." The definitive study of British population structure found that most of central and southern England forms a single genetic cluster containing nearly half the sample — and that took recruiting rural people with all four grandparents born within fifty miles of each other. Consumer ethnicity percentages are much cruder than that. Do not treat "12% Scottish" as a fact about your tree.

It also will not reach your colonial immigrants. Genealogical ancestors double every generation, but genetic ancestors grow only linearly — by about nine generations back it is quite likely that any specific ancestor contributed none of your DNA at all. Your immigrant ancestors sit at roughly generations eight to eleven. Most of them, genetically speaking, are simply not in you.

What it will do is match you to relatives — and for someone with roots this concentrated, that list will be long, because these families intermarried locally for six generations. Matches plus documented trees are how brick walls actually fall.

If you test, in this order

  • Finish the free document work first. The 1880 census asked every person's parents' birthplaces. Pull it for every line. My prediction, which you can falsify cheaply: it will read Georgia, North Carolina, South Carolina and Virginia, with no foreign country anywhere — confirming colonial arrival.
  • One autosomal test, at AncestryDNA — not for the pie chart, which you should largely ignore, but for the match database, which is much the largest.
  • Then Y-DNA, if you want the Yawn question answered. That is the highest-value single test in this tree: it is the only realistic way to settle a surname the standard reference calls "unexplained." It needs a man carrying the Yawn name in an unbroken father-to-father line, so you would need to recruit a cousin.
  • Chase the rare names, not the common ones. Cribb, Ricketson, Yawn and Giddens are rare and geographically concentrated. Brown, Hall and Harper are not, and time spent on them without a documented anchor is wasted.

The answer, plainly

If the question is "what country am I from," it does not quite fit this family. They were not recent immigrants from anywhere. They were Carolina people — herders and small farmers of mostly English and partly Highland Scots descent, with at least one Flemish and possibly one German thread, who had been on this continent for a century or more before Georgia was a state.

That is the answer. The English village and the Scottish glen are real, but they lie on the far side of a documentary gap that, for plain folk of this kind, is usually unbridgeable without DNA.

SHIPS

Can we find the ship?

the honest answer, and the real route

Almost certainly not, and the reason is a single date. The United States kept no federal record of who arrived until the Steerage Act of 1819, which took effect on 1 January 1820. Only then were ships' masters required to hand customs a list of every passenger with name, age, sex, occupation, country of origin and destination.

Ellis Island, Castle Garden, the famous manifests people picture — all of them are 1820 or later. Every immigrant ancestor in this tree had been in America for fifty to a hundred and fifty years before the first of those lists was written.

Where the arrival was not

The Georgia Archives' colonial collections — Colonial Wills, Colonial Will Books, Colonial Estate Records, Colonial Plats and Warrants, Headright and Bounty Plats — were searched for Luke, McKinnon, Graves and Ricketson. The result is a clean negative: not one of these families appears in colonial Georgia. The handful of matches are men whose given name was Luke, and Graveses with no traceable link.

That matters, because it means nobody in this tree stepped off a ship in Georgia. They arrived overland, from the Carolinas, a generation or more after the Revolution. Whatever port the family first entered, it was in Virginia, the Carolinas or Pennsylvania, and it was long before anyone was writing the names down.

The one line with a named migration behind it

McKinnon is a Highland name — Clan MacKinnon, of Skye and Mull — and James Malcolm McKinnon Sr. was born in North Carolina about 1804. That points at the Cape Fear valley, where Gaelic-speaking Highlanders settled in such numbers that by the 1770s they were a third of the upper valley's population and the country was called the Valley of the Scots. The migration begins with the Argyll Colony, which sailed from Scotland in June 1739 and reached North Carolina that September under Duncan Campbell, Neill, Daniel and Dugald McNeill, and Coll McAlister.

But that is a context, not an ancestor. This tree stops at a man born in 1804 whose parents are unknown. Between him and any ship lie three or four unproven generations. Naming the vessel would mean inventing the link, and this file does not do that.

What would actually have to happen

The order cannot be reversed. You find the immigrant first, then the ship — never the other way round. The furthest back any line here reaches is Benjamin Graves, born before 1774 in Brunswick County, North Carolina; Timothy Ricketson Sr., before 1760; Daniel Luke, about 1783. Every one of those men was already American-born. The immigrant is still one to three generations beyond the end of the evidence.

For pre-1820 arrivals the records that do exist are not passenger lists at all. They are headright and land grants (colonies granted acreage per person transported, so the grant sometimes names who came), colonial wills and estate records, naturalisations, and ship lists for particular organised migrations like the Argyll Colony — useful only once you already know which migration your family joined.

Which leaves DNA, and it is not a consolation prize. For ordinary farming families who crossed before records began, testing is not a shortcut around the research — it is the only instrument that reaches further back than the paper does.

A Y-DNA test follows the strict father-to-son line — Luke, in this case — and matches it against others of the same surname, which can place the line in a region of Britain or Ireland and sometimes join it to a documented family. An autosomal test covers every line at once and finds living cousins, whose trees frequently reach past the point where yours stops. Both are in the region of a hundred dollars, which is four death certificates.
