The Civil War Nine men from these families, read out of the pension applications they swore themselves. What they did, how it ended for them, and what the same papers say about how they lived for the fifty years afterwards.

What these men were fighting for should be said first, because the documents will not say it. The Confederate States existed to keep four million people in slavery, and its own founding documents said so without embarrassment. Every man on this page enlisted in that cause. Nothing below is written to soften that, and the forms they filled in — printed by the State of Georgia decades later — were part of a long public effort to remember the war as something else.

And the second thing the papers show is poverty. Almost every file here is an indigent pension: Georgia paid these men only once they had sworn, before the Ordinary of their county, that they owned nothing at all. They are applications to be certified as destitute. Most of the men signed them with a mark because they could not write their names.

I

Three families, one regiment

1861 – 1865

The 29th Georgia Infantry was raised out of this exact corner of the state — Lowndes, Clinch, Coffee and the counties against them. Three surnames from this tree turn up in three different companies of it, which is what a locally raised regiment looks like from below: not an army someone joined, but the men from the next farm over.

III

What the papers say as a group

1891 – 1919

Nobody here came home to anything. Ricketson: "I have nothing." Newbern: property none, supported "chiefly by help of children and friends." Harper: "I have my wife and six small children. They have no means of support." Wilson died on the roll at sixty dollars a year and the county tax books showed him and his wife returning nothing at all. The one man who lists any property is a widow — Helen Wilson, fifteen head of cattle, a hundred and fifty dollars, every other line blank.

Two of them were in the same prison. John P. Ricketson was "captured, carried to Point Lookout in M.D." E. W. Overstreet spent the last six months of the war there too, taken at Savannah the day the city fell. Point Lookout, on the Maryland shore, was the largest Union prison camp of the war.

The widows' files are the better documents. A soldier's application asks what he did and what he owns. A widow's asks when she married, where, who can swear to it, and when he died — which is how Helen Wilson's file produced a wedding date of 8 August 1867 and a death of 13 September 1912 that no other record on this site carried.

And one line on Helen's file explains a whole county. The form demanded proof of marriage. The clerk wrote across the foot of it: "No record of Marriage. Records all destroyed by fire in 1868." Clinch County's courthouse burned, which is why everything on that branch thins the moment it crosses back before 1870, and why a neighbour had to swear from memory to a wedding twenty-four years earlier.

IV

Did any of them serve the Union?

an open check

Every man found so far is Confederate, and that is partly an artifact of where the looking has been done: a Georgia Confederate pension application, by definition, required Confederate service. Searching that collection can only ever return Confederates.

The honest answer on the Union side is "not checked properly yet." The definitive index is the National Park Service's Civil War Soldiers and Sailors System, which carries both armies and labels each man's side — 4.19 million Union records against 2.05 million Confederate, with 244,142 for Georgia. It is reachable, but its search has to be driven through its own form rather than by a web address, so a surname-by-surname sweep of this tree against it is a job still to do.

What can be said in the meantime is about the odds. Georgia raised almost no white Union troops. The only white Georgia unit in Federal service of any size was the 1st Georgia Infantry Battalion (US), organised in 1864 and recruited overwhelmingly from Unionists in the north Georgia mountains — a different world from the wiregrass counties these families farmed. On present evidence it would be surprising to find one of these men in it, though south Georgia had plenty of quieter disaffection: desertion in the pine barrens was common enough by 1864 that the state sent troops after it.

Georgia's real contribution to the Union armies was Black. Thousands of men who had been enslaved in this state enlisted in the United States Colored Troops once they reached Federal lines. Every household on this tree is recorded White in every census that asks, so none of these particular men served in those regiments.

Which raises the question this page should not dodge. The families here were white farmers in a slave state, and the relevant record — the 1860 and 1850 slave schedules, which list slaveholders by name and county — has not been searched for any of them. That is a separate and more uncomfortable piece of work than counting regiments, and it is the one that would actually connect these people to what the war was about. It is on the list and it has not been done.
V

Where these came from, and what is still unread

method

Georgia's Confederate pension applications are free and fully digitised at the Georgia Archives Virtual Vault, and they are searchable by surname with the county attached to each file. Every surname on this tree was run against the collection and filtered to the fourteen counties this family occupies.

That returned 150 files. Nine have been read. The rest are listed in PENSION-SWEEP.md in the project repository, with the search recipe at the top — Merritt (eight files in Coffee), Spivey (six), Hughs, Sirmans, Brown, Willis, Durham, and more Overstreets and Giddenses.
